GRAMMAR/PUNCTUATION PASS (do this for every text field, not just hook/bridge): scan hook_slide, bridge_slide, hook_pop_phrase, bridge_pop_phrase, every body_slides "text", recap_slide, cta_slide, cta_promise, and caption for: a missing capital at the start of a sentence or fragment; a proper noun lowercased anywhere in the sentence ("google ads," "meta," "instagram" instead of "Google Ads," "Meta," "Instagram" — this applies mid-sentence too, not just sentence-initial); mixed straight/curly quote styles within the same field; double spaces, double punctuation, or a sentence that just trails off without ending punctuation. Fix these silently as part of your normal edit pass — they don't need a rewrite of the whole line, just the specific error corrected.

SIMPLICITY & WARMTH PASS (do this first, before the numbered checks below): read every hook_slide, bridge_slide, and body_slides "text" and flag two things on sight —
(a) Harshness: does any line talk down to the reader, imply they're failing, or use shame words
("you're wasting," "your mistake," "you're ignoring," "killing your results")? If so, rewrite it
to describe what happens rather than accuse the reader — see the content brain's SIMPLICITY &
WARMTH BAR and FRAME AROUND THE INSIGHT sections for the exact swap pattern.
(b) Density: is any body_slides "text" over 16 words, or does it chain two ideas together with
"which means," "that's usually where," or a semicolon? If so, cut it to one idea in one short
sentence — a shorter, slightly less complete slide beats a long, thorough one every time under
this pass. Same check for hook_slide/bridge_slide against the 8-word ceiling in check 3 below.

For every carousel, check the hook_slide and bridge_slide against this bar:
1. Would a marketing-curious reader stop scrolling for THIS exact line — not "would this get some engagement" in general?
2. Does it state a real, specific mechanic or fact with a genuine information gap, rather than a vague tease or a personal accusation aimed at "your" business?
3. Count the words in hook_slide by splitting on spaces — an actual count, not a feel-based guess. 8 or fewer is a hard pass/fail line: 9+ is an automatic fail, no exceptions, even if the line reads fine out loud. Is it also one sentence, with no dash-linked second clause tacked onto the end, and free of hedging words (basically, essentially, kind of)?
4. Does it sound different from every other hook in this same batch — different opener word, different sentence shape, different angle execution than the other 4?
5. Does it lead with a real-world outcome the reader can picture (money, customers, time, a fixed problem) rather than a bare, floating stat? A hook that's just a percentage with no human consequence attached fails this even if the number is real and specific — see the content brain's BENEFIT OVER RAW STAT rule. Reword it so the number illustrates the outcome instead of standing in for it.
6. Does it avoid narrow small-business-vertical framing (cafés, gyms, dentists, hairdressers, plumbers, a named local place)? If a hook or example leans on one of these, generalize it — see the content brain's GENERAL, NOT HYPER-LOCAL rule.
7. Does hook_slide actually name its platform (Google Ads / Meta Ads / Instagram / email — or, for the AI Tools niche, a named tool like ChatGPT/Claude/Zapier or the word "AI" itself) in the line itself, not just imply it? A hook that's specific and sharp but never says which platform or tool it's about still fails — add the name without inflating the line past 8 words (matching check 3 above — this line previously said 10, which contradicted it), even if that means tightening something else in the same breath.
8. Across the batch of 5: are at most 1-2 hooks number-led? If 3 or more hooks in the batch lead with a percentage/€/$ figure as the main hook, that's a failure of angle variety even if each individual hook is well-written — rewrite the weakest number-led ones into outcome/mistake/realization hooks instead.
9. Does hook_slide or bridge_slide use unexplained platform jargon — CPM, ROAS, CPC, CPL, pixel, lookalike audience, Quality Score, attribution, impressions, frequency, conversion, auction, bidding, "learning phase," "smart bidding," "low intent"/"high intent," or a similar insider term — without simplifying it per the content brain's PLAIN-ENGLISH SWAP LIST? A term that would make a reader with zero ads experience pause and re-read is a fail, even if it's technically accurate. Rewrite using the plain-English version, applying the same swap logic to any jargon term not explicitly on that list.

If a hook or bridge fails ANY of these, rewrite it. Keep the carousel's assigned angle and niche, but make the line itself land harder. When you rewrite, resist the first fix that comes to mind — that's usually the same shape as whatever's already overused. Push for the version that sounds like a sharp person said it once, off the cuff, not a marketer workshopping a headline in a meeting.

Every carousel also has "hook_pop_phrase" and "bridge_pop_phrase" — short (2-5 word) exact substrings of hook_slide/bridge_slide that the renderer blows up to giant size as the visual hook when there's no €/$/% figure to pull out instead. Check both: they must be a verbatim substring (same casing, same punctuation) of their parent line, and they should be the single most punchy, consequence-carrying words in that line — not a connector, not a generic noun. If you rewrite a hook_slide or bridge_slide, you MUST also update its pop_phrase to match the new wording (a pop_phrase that no longer appears in the line breaks the render, same failure mode as a stale "keyword" on a body slide). If a carousel is missing hook_pop_phrase or bridge_pop_phrase entirely, add one.

Also spot-check body_slides against the one-clear-idea bar: each slide is a {"text": ..., "keyword": ...} object. The "text" should be ONE short, plain sentence a reader could repeat to a friend after one pass — 6-14 words is the target, 16 is the hard ceiling. "Switch to phrase match" fails not because it's short, but because it gives an instruction with zero reason attached — the fix is a short reason, not a longer sentence: "Phrase match only shows for searches that mean it" (9 words) clears the bar just fine. If "text" is over 16 words or chains two ideas with "which means"/"that's usually where"/a semicolon, cut it down to the single more useful idea rather than keeping both. Leave body slides alone if they already clear the bar — don't rewrite for the sake of rewriting.

If you rewrite a slide's "text" in a way that removes or changes its "keyword" substring, update "keyword" to match — it must remain an exact, verbatim substring of the new "text" (same casing, same punctuation), 1-4 words, naming the number/setting/mechanic the slide is actually about. A "keyword" that no longer appears in "text" breaks the rendered highlight, so this is not optional cleanup.

Some body_slides objects (before-after format only) also carry "before" and "after" keys, and some (comparison format only, first body slide only) carry "compare_a" and "compare_b" keys — both pairs render as their own dedicated visual above the slide's text. Preserve whichever pair is present as-is when you edit that slide's "text" — do not drop them, and do not add either pair to a slide that didn't already have it (that decision belongs to the draft, not to you).

COHERENCE PASS (new — do this for every carousel, separate from the hook/body checks above): read the throughline field, then read hook_slide, bridge_slide, and all 6 body_slides in order as one continuous piece, the way an actual reader swipes through them. Check:

3. Does the hook's information gap stay open past the bridge, or does the carousel accidentally pay off its whole promise by slide 3-4 with nothing left driving slides 5-9? If the back half feels like padding after the real answer already landed, tighten the early slides so they raise the question without fully answering it.

Swipe-risk check (new): read body_slides 1 through 5 (not slide 6) one at a time and ask, "if someone stopped scrolling right after reading only this slide, would they feel like they'd already gotten the full point?" Per SWIPE COPY CUES, each of those 5 slides needs some form of forward motion — an explicit pointer, an open question, a teased comparison, or a connective opener that clearly implies more is coming. If a slide fully closes its own thought with nothing left dangling, add a forward-leaning close per the techniques in SWIPE COPY CUES, picking a different technique than whatever the neighboring slides already used so the batch doesn't read as a formula.
